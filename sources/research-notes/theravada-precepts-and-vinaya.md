# Theravada precepts and Vinaya

## Scope

This note supports cautious reader-facing pages about ethical training and monastic discipline in Theravada Buddhism.

It distinguishes lay five precepts, lay eight-precept observance, novice ten precepts, and the Pāṭimokkha for fully ordained monastics. It is not an ordination manual, legal ruling, substitute for a qualified Vinaya teacher, or comprehensive history of Theravada institutions.

## Do not collapse “precepts” and “Vinaya”

Japanese “戒律” can blur several different Pāli concepts.

- **sīla**: ethical virtue, moral discipline, bodily and verbal conduct, or a set of ethical observances depending on context.
- **sikkhāpada**: a training rule or training principle that one undertakes.
- **Vinaya**: the broader body of monastic discipline, procedures, origin stories, allowances, and communal rules.
- **Pāṭimokkha**: the core recitation code of offences and training rules for fully ordained monastics within the Vinaya.

Reader-facing content should use “戒” for ethical training and “律” for monastic discipline when the distinction matters.

## Five precepts

The five precepts are the basic lay training set:

1. abstaining from taking life;
2. abstaining from taking what is not given;
3. abstaining from sexual misconduct;
4. abstaining from false speech;
5. abstaining from intoxicants that are a basis for heedlessness.

The formula is an undertaking of training. The checked sources do not support presenting it as a command imposed by an external deity.

## Eight precepts

The eight precepts are commonly undertaken by laypeople on Uposatha days or during periods of intensive practice.

They retain the first five with one major change: the third becomes complete abstinence from sexual activity. They then add:

6. abstaining from eating after the permitted time, conventionally after noon until the next dawn;
7. abstaining from dancing, singing, music, shows, garlands, perfumes, cosmetics, and bodily adornment;
8. abstaining from high or luxurious beds and seats.

The eight-precept set is more renunciant than the ordinary five-precept lay set. It should not be presented as the routine minimum for all lay Buddhists or as a ranking of moral worth.

## Ten precepts

The ten precepts are commonly presented as the training rules for novice monks and nuns.

They are closely related to the eight precepts, but:

- entertainment and adornment are separated into two rules;
- abstaining from accepting gold and silver, conventionally explained as money, is added as the tenth.

Reader-facing pages should not treat the tenth precept as a simple personal-finance recommendation for lay readers. Its meaning belongs to novice and monastic discipline and varies in modern implementation.

## Vinaya and Pāṭimokkha

The Pāṭimokkha is the central recitation code within the broader Vinaya system.

In the Theravada recension, the checked reference materials state:

- 227 Pāṭimokkha rules for fully ordained bhikkhus;
- 311 Pāṭimokkha rules for fully ordained bhikkhunīs.

These figures should not be described as the total number of every Vinaya instruction. The wider Vinaya includes explanatory narratives, definitions, exceptions, allowances, communal procedures, ordination procedures, and rules preserved outside the bare Pāṭimokkha list.

## Main Pāṭimokkha categories

The bhikkhu and bhikkhunī codes contain graded categories whose consequences differ.

- **Pārājika**: defeats, entailing loss of affiliation with the ordained community.
- **Saṅghādisesa**: serious offences requiring formal communal procedures.
- **Aniyata**: indefinite rules; present in the bhikkhu code.
- **Nissaggiya Pācittiya**: offences involving forfeiture and confession.
- **Pācittiya**: offences involving confession.
- **Pāṭidesanīya**: offences involving acknowledgement.
- **Sekhiya**: training rules, many concerning comportment, food, and teaching.
- **Adhikaraṇa-samatha**: procedures for settling disputes.

A short reader-facing overview may describe these categories, but it should not attempt to adjudicate actual cases. Vinaya interpretation depends on wording, intention, perception, circumstances, exceptions, procedures, and community authority.

## Bhikkhunī material

The checked Bhikkhunī Pāṭimokkha translation states that the Theravada bhikkhunī code contains 311 rules and explains substantial overlap and difference relative to the bhikkhu code.

Questions about the historical development of particular rules, the eight garudhammas, ordination lineage, contemporary restoration, and institutional authority are debated and require dedicated sources. Commonplace should not settle those disputes in a short overview.

## Editorial boundaries

- Keep canonical text, commentarial interpretation, modern monastic practice, and Commonplace interpretation visibly separate.
- Do not infer clinical, psychological, legal, nutritional, or addiction-treatment effects from precept texts.
- Do not tell readers to adopt eight-precept fasting without considering health, medication, age, pregnancy, eating-disorder risk, and qualified guidance.
- Do not use rule counts to rank traditions, genders, communities, or individuals.
- Do not present the Pāṭimokkha as a list of lifestyle tips for laypeople.
- Do not reproduce detailed offence adjudication or ordination instructions in a short public page.

## Remaining uncertainty

- Compare AN 8.41, AN 8.43, and additional Uposatha translations directly.
- Review the Khuddakapāṭha ten-training-rule passage across multiple translations.
- Verify current Theravada community explanations of novice money rules and practical allowances.
- Add specialist scholarship on the history and structure of the Pāli Vinaya and bhikkhunī ordination debates.
- Review Japanese translations of Pārājika, Saṅghādisesa, Pācittiya, and related technical categories.
